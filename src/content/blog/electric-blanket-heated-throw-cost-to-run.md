## Why a throw is so much cheaper than heating the house

Central heat has to warm the **air, walls, furniture, and every leak** in the home to keep you comfortable. A heated throw puts a small amount of heat directly against the person who is cold, under an insulating layer of fleece.

Rough orders of magnitude (check your own equipment):

- **Heated throw (50×60 in):** commonly rated around 60–200 W on high; lower settings cycle the element, so average draw is often less.
- **Full/queen electric blanket:** often roughly 100–200 W on high, more for king sizes with dual controls.
- **Plug-in space heater:** up to 1,500 W on high (the standard US 120 V limit for a portable heater on a typical circuit).
- **Electric baseboard or resistance strips:** often 1,000–2,000 W per room heater; heat pump aux strips are commonly 5–15 kW.
- **Heat pump or furnace:** thousands of watts of heat delivered to the whole house, whether anyone is in a given room or not.

Every resistive heater — throw, blanket, space heater, baseboard — turns essentially 100% of its electricity into heat. The throw doesn't win because it's "more efficient"; it wins because it **heats far less stuff**.

## The math: watts × hours × rate

The formula is the same for any plug-in heater:

**Cost = (watts ÷ 1,000) × hours × your rate per kWh**

Your rate is the **total** price per kWh on your bill (energy plus delivery, divided by kWh used). The US average residential price has been running roughly 17–18¢/kWh in 2026, but yours may be 11¢ or 40¢ — use your own.

### Example cost per evening and per season (illustrative, $0.17/kWh)

| Setup | Assumed avg. draw | Per 4-hour evening | Per season (120 evenings) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heated throw, low setting | ~40 W | ~0.16 kWh ≈ 3¢ | ~19 kWh ≈ $3 |
| Heated throw, high setting | ~100 W | ~0.4 kWh ≈ 7¢ | ~48 kWh ≈ $8 |
| Throw on high, 200 W label, no cycling | 200 W | ~0.8 kWh ≈ 14¢ | ~96 kWh ≈ $16 |
| Queen electric blanket on the couch, high | ~150 W | ~0.6 kWh ≈ 10¢ | ~72 kWh ≈ $12 |
| Space heater on low | ~750 W | ~3 kWh ≈ 51¢ | ~360 kWh ≈ $61 |
| Space heater on high | 1,500 W | ~6 kWh ≈ $1.02 | ~720 kWh ≈ $122 |

These are **examples, not measurements of any particular product.** Your throw's label wattage is a maximum; the controller usually switches the element on and off to hold the setting, so average draw on low or medium is often a fraction of the label. A plug-in meter will tell you the truth in one evening.

Takeaway: even used heavily, a heated throw is usually a **single-digit-to-low-teens dollar line item for the whole winter**. The question that matters is what it lets you do with the thermostat.

## What a thermostat setback is actually worth

The heated throw's job is to make a lower setpoint comfortable. The savings come from the **house heating less**, not from the throw.

What the evidence supports:

- The US Department of Energy says you can save **up to about 10% a year** on heating and cooling by turning the thermostat back **7–10°F for 8 hours a day** from its normal setting.
- Savings scale roughly with **how many degrees** and **how many hours** you set back, and with how cold your climate is. A 3°F setback for a 4-hour evening is worth much less than an 8°F overnight setback.
- Bigger setbacks pay more in leaky homes and cold climates, because heat loss is proportional to the indoor-outdoor temperature difference.

### Example: a modest evening setback (illustrative only)

Suppose your home spends about **$150 a month heating** in winter and you normally hold 70°F in the evening. You drop to 67°F from 6–10 pm and use a throw on the couch. A rough rule of thumb of about **1% of heating cost per degree for 8 hours** would put a 3°F, 4-hour setback around 1.5% — on the order of **$2–3 a month** — minus maybe **$1–2 a month** for the throw. Net: **a few dollars a month**, more if you also set back overnight.

That's not dramatic, and it isn't guaranteed. Where the combination really shines:

- **Electric resistance heat or heat pump aux strips.** Every degree you don't ask for avoids kilowatts of expensive heat — see [heat pump aux heat settings](/blog/heat-pump-aux-heat-high-bill-thermostat-settings/) for how setback recovery can trigger aux heat if you're not careful.
- **One cold person, warm household.** Instead of heating the house to the coldest person's comfort level, warm that person.
- **A habit you already have.** If the thermostat already drops at night, a throw lets you start the setback earlier in the evening.

### Heat pump caveat

With a heat pump, a deep setback followed by a fast recovery can kick on **auxiliary resistance heat**, which can erase the savings. Keep evening setbacks modest (2–4°F), use a thermostat with smart recovery or aux lockout, and check your own bill and thermostat runtime reports.

## Worked examples by heating type (illustrative)

The same 3°F evening setback is worth very different amounts depending on what heats your home. These examples assume a 4-hour evening, a throw averaging 80 W (about 5¢ a night at $0.17/kWh), and rough heating-load guesses for a mid-size home on a 35°F evening. Your numbers will differ — use them to see **which homes benefit most**, not as a forecast.

| Home heating | Rough heat input avoided by a 3°F, 4-hour setback | Energy price assumed | Rough value per evening | Throw cost | Rough net per evening |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electric baseboard / resistance | ~1.5–2.5 kWh | $0.17/kWh | ~25–43¢ | ~5¢ | ~20–38¢ |
| Heat pump (no aux), COP ~2.5–3 | ~0.5–1 kWh | $0.17/kWh | ~9–17¢ | ~5¢ | ~4–12¢ |
| Gas furnace, ~95% AFUE | ~0.05–0.09 therm | $1.50/therm | ~8–14¢ | ~5¢ | ~3–9¢ |
| Heat pump that triggers aux on recovery | Can be zero or negative | — | Often ~0 | ~5¢ | Can be a loss |

What this tells you:

- **Electric resistance homes** get the clearest win: every degree you skip avoids expensive kWh one-for-one.
- **Heat pump and gas homes** still come out ahead with a throw, but the margin per evening is small — dimes, not dollars. It adds up over a winter of evenings and pairs well with an overnight setback.
- **A heat pump whose recovery triggers aux strips** can wipe out the savings. Shallow setbacks and a thermostat with smart recovery or an aux lockout temperature fix that.
- **Replacing a space heater** beats all of these: dropping a 1,500 W heater for a throw saves roughly **$0.90–1.00 per 4-hour evening** at $0.17/kWh, regardless of how the house is heated.

## Who it's for (and who it isn't)

**Good fit:**

- One person who's always colder than everyone else in the house.
- Remote workers at a desk in a cool home office during the day — warm the person, not the whole house while it's empty otherwise.
- Renters who can't add insulation or change the heating system but control a thermostat.
- Anyone currently running a space heater next to the couch.
- Older homes with drafty living rooms where the thermostat sits in a warmer hallway.

**Poor fit:**

- Households that won't lower the thermostat — the throw becomes added load.
- Homes with infants, toddlers, or curious chewing pets in the living area, unless it's stored and unplugged when not in direct supervised use.
- People who will fall asleep under it nightly — get a product designed for overnight sleep.
- Anyone who needs warm ambient air for health reasons; personal heat doesn't replace a safe room temperature.

## Measure first (~5 steps)

1. **Find your rate.** Divide your last bill's total electric charges by kWh used.
2. **Plug the throw into a Kill A Watt** (or similar plug-in meter) and reset it.
3. **Use the throw normally for one evening** on your usual setting — preheat on high, then drop to low or medium if that's what you do.
4. **Read total kWh** after the evening and multiply by your rate. That's your real cost per night.
5. **Check your thermostat.** Note your usual evening setpoint, then try 2–3°F lower with the throw for a week. Compare heating runtime in your thermostat app or your utility's daily usage chart if it has one.

If you already own a meter from measuring a [fridge](/blog/refrigerator-freezer-cost-to-run-thermometer-coil-brush/) or [dehumidifier](/blog/dehumidifier-cost-to-run-basement-humidity/), this is a five-minute job.

## Free fixes before you buy anything

- **Lower the setpoint 1–2°F and add a sweater and socks.** Clothing is free zone heating.
- **Use the throw you already own.** A plain fleece blanket plus a lower setpoint is the zero-dollar version of this whole guide.
- **Move the couch away from drafty windows and exterior doors.** Sitting in a draft makes 68°F feel like 64°F. See [window insulation kits](/blog/window-insulation-kits-winter-drafts/), [weatherstripping](/blog/best-weatherstripping-draft-stoppers/), and [thermal curtains](/blog/thermal-blackout-curtains-energy-savings/).
- **Close curtains at dusk** on cold nights and open them on sunny afternoons.
- **Schedule the setback** so you don't forget — a programmable or [smart thermostat](/blog/best-smart-thermostats-2026/) does it every night.
- **Run a ceiling fan on low, reverse (clockwise) only if it actually feels warmer** in rooms with tall ceilings; skip it if it makes you colder.

## Should you buy one? A quick decision tree

1. **Do you already lower the thermostat in the evening — or are you willing to?** If **no**, a heated throw adds a few cents a night and saves nothing. Buy it for comfort, not your bill.
2. **Is it one or two people who get cold?** If **yes**, a throw is the cheapest targeted heat there is. If the whole family is cold, look at drafts and the thermostat schedule first.
3. **Are you using a space heater to warm the couch area now?** If **yes**, switching to a throw for solo evenings is the clearest win in this guide — often **50 cents to a dollar a night** saved at typical rates.
4. **Will anyone sleep under it?** If **yes**, buy a product designed and labeled for overnight bedding (or a heated mattress pad), not a couch throw.
5. **Kids, pets, or someone who can't feel heat well?** Read the safety section below first; for some households the answer is a plain blanket.
6. **Still yes?** Pick a UL- or ETL-listed throw with **auto shut-off**, **multiple heat settings**, and a **detachable, washable** design.

## What to look for when buying

- **Safety listing:** a recognized testing mark such as **UL** or **ETL** on the product and controller.
- **Auto shut-off:** commonly 3–4 hours on throws, sometimes adjustable up to 8–10 hours on some brands. Shorter is safer on a couch.
- **Several heat settings:** you'll usually preheat on high and run on low.
- **Detachable controller and cord** so the blanket can be machine-washed per the label.
- **Cord length** that reaches an outlet without stretching across a walkway.
- **Size:** 50×60 in covers one adult; bigger is heavier and costs a bit more to run.

## First-use tips and troubleshooting

- **Preheat on high for 10–30 minutes, then drop to low.** Most of the comfort comes from the first warm-up; holding on high all evening mostly costs more and runs hotter.
- **Throw feels unevenly warm?** Shake it out flat. Bunched areas can feel hotter; cold spots that never warm after flattening can mean a failed wire — stop using it.
- **Controller blinks or shows an error code:** unplug, check that the connector to the blanket is fully seated, then consult the manual. Repeated errors mean replace, not repair.
- **It shut off mid-movie:** that's the auto shut-off doing its job. Turn it back on if you're awake; don't look for ways to bypass it.
- **Faint smell on first use** can be normal for some new products per the manual; a burning smell or discoloration is not — unplug immediately.
- **Washing:** detach the controller and cord, follow the care label (often gentle cycle, cool water, low dry or line dry), and make sure the connector is fully dry before plugging back in.
- **Storage:** roll loosely or fold gently per the manual when it's off and cool; avoid sharp creases and storing heavy items on top.

## Safety do-nots

Heated bedding is low-wattage, but it puts electrical heat right against your body. Follow the manual, and:

- **Don't fold, bunch, or tuck it** under cushions, mattresses, or yourself while it's on. Drape it flat on top of you.
- **Don't sit or lie on it** or pile heavy items on top. Pinched or kinked wires can overheat.
- **Don't use it on infants, young children, or anyone who can't feel heat, operate the controller, or remove it themselves** — including some people with diabetes, neuropathy, or limited mobility, and anyone sedated or very sleepy. Check the manual's age and medical warnings.
- **Don't let pets chew, knead, or nest on it.** Claws and teeth can damage wiring; keep it out of pet beds and crates.
- **Don't defeat the auto shut-off** or run a couch throw all night unless the manual explicitly allows it.
- **Don't use it wet,** and don't run the controller through the wash.
- **Don't run cords under rugs or furniture,** and plug directly into a wall outlet rather than daisy-chained power strips.
- **Don't use a damaged or very old unit.** Unplug and retire it if you see scorch marks, exposed wiring, stiff or brittle spots, or a controller that flashes an error.
- **Don't use it with an adjustable bed, waterbed, or on a hospital bed** unless the manufacturer says it's compatible.

## Honest limits

- **The throw doesn't save money by itself.** The thermostat setback does. No setback, no savings.
- **Savings are modest for short evening setbacks** — often a few dollars a month. Overnight setbacks and electric resistance heat move the needle more.
- **Our cost table is illustrative.** Label wattage, cycling, and settings vary by model; measure yours.
- **Comfort matters.** If a setback makes someone in the home uncomfortable or is a health concern (older adults, infants, certain medical conditions), don't push it. Keep indoor temperatures safe.
- **Payback is about comfort.** A $40 throw used to replace a space heater pays back quickly; one used on top of an unchanged thermostat never pays back on energy.
- **We don't guarantee any savings.** Climate, fuel, home, and habits decide the result.
