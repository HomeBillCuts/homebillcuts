“Vampire power” (standby load) is real: chargers, game consoles, AV receivers, streaming boxes, printers, and secondary gadgets draw watts while “off.” Smart plugs and smart strips can automate cutoffs — but they’re tools, not magic. This guide covers **when smart plugs are worth it**, how to measure first, and a short list of product types that fit US 120V homes.

## What vampire power is (and isn’t)

Standby load is the continuous draw of devices that stay partially powered for remotes, clocks, network wake, or poor design. Killing it helps most when:

- You have a cluster of AV gear that stays warm all day.
- An old game console, DVR, or PC rarely sleeps deeply.
- Space heaters, irons, or curling wands deserve a hard OFF when idle (safety + energy).
- You’re on a high $/kWh rate or a small apartment where always-on loads are noticeable.

It helps less when:

- You’ve already put entertainment centers on a mechanical switch strip you actually use.
- The big bill drivers are HVAC, water heating, or EVs — plugs won’t fix those.
- You “automate” outlets then leave everything set to Always On.

**Honest framing:** A 5–15W standby cluster is worth hunting; it is rarely the same scale as a leaky duct or a heat-pump aux strip. Measure before you buy a dozen plugs.

## Measure first (cheap truth serum)

1. Use a plug-in energy meter on suspect devices for 24–72 hours.
2. Note watts while “off,” while idle, and while in use.
3. Multiply idle watts × 24 × 30 / 1000 × your rate for a rough monthly cost.
4. Decide if a $15–30 smart plug is justified — or if a $10 mechanical strip you’ll flip is enough.

Whole-home mysteries still belong to interval data or a [home energy monitor comparison](/blog/emporia-vue-vs-sense/).

## Safety limits you should not ignore

> **Safety notes:** Smart plugs are for **cord-and-plug 120V loads within the plug’s rating** (read amps/watts on the device). Do **not** use cheap plugs on space heaters, window A/Cs, sump pumps, medical devices, refrigerators with needed defrost cycles, or anything above the rated load. Avoid daisy-chaining power strips. Keep plugs away from moisture unless rated for it. Smart plugs are not AFCI/GFCI substitutes. For hardwired loads, hire an electrician — don’t fake it with outlet adapters. If a plug feels hot, stop using it.

Also:

- Don’t cut power mid-update on fragile electronics if you care about corruption — schedule wisely.
- Some devices (cable modems, security NVRs, network gear) should stay on unless you have a deliberate plan.
- Motor loads and high inrush devices need plugs rated for that duty.

## Features that matter for bill-cutting

Prioritize:

1. **Reliable schedules / timers** you’ll keep enabled
2. **Energy monitoring** on the plug (optional but educational)
3. **Physical button** on the plug for guests and outages
4. **UL/ETL listing** and realistic amp ratings
5. **App that doesn’t require a circus of subscriptions** for basic on/off

Deprioritize: rainbow light shows, voice gimmicks as a “savings feature,” and ecosystems you’ll abandon in six months.

## Automation patterns that work

1. **Nightly cutoff** for gaming PCs’ accessory outlets (not necessarily the NAS).
2. **Away mode** for hobby soldering irons and 3D-printer accessory loads (respect cool-down needs).
3. **Daytime OFF** for decorative lighting you already [LEDified](/blog/led-bulbs-save-money-wattage-guide/).
4. **One-hour auto-off** for bathroom fans’ accessory heaters — only within plug ratings.

Pair with HVAC habits and [draft sealing](/blog/best-weatherstripping-draft-stoppers/) so you’re not optimizing 8W while the attic hatch dumps conditioned air.

## Bottom line

Smart plugs earn their keep when they **reliably cut measured standby loads** you’d otherwise leave on 24/7 — especially AV clusters and charger nests. They do not replace thermostat discipline, weatherization, or honest bill reading. Measure, stay inside electrical ratings, prefer listed hardware, and skip savings guarantees.
