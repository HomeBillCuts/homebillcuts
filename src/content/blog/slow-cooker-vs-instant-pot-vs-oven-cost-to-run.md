October is chili, stew, and pot-roast season, and it's when people start asking whether the slow cooker, the Instant Pot, or the oven is the cheap way to cook a big pot of food. The honest answer is that the gap between them is usually cents per meal, not dollars. The most wasteful common habit is running a big oven for a single pot, and a pressure cooker usually uses the least. Below is the math at three common electric rates, what popular dishes actually cost, a few example households, and when buying a new cooker makes sense.

## The short version

- A pressure cooker usually uses the least electricity per pot, often 5–10 cents at $0.17/kWh.
- A slow cooker is close behind: roughly 17–34 cents for an all-day cook.
- A single-pot electric oven braise costs the most, roughly 35–70 cents.
- A lidded pot on a low gas flame is often cheapest of all if you already have gas.
- The ingredients cost far more than the energy. A $12 pot of chili costs maybe 25 cents to cook.
- The cheapest wins come from habits: batch the oven when it's on, keep the lid on, and skip long Warm holds.

## Why the appliances use different amounts of energy

Every braise needs the food brought up to a simmer and held there. What you pay for is everything else.

- An oven heats a large insulated box (often 4–5 cubic feet), plus racks and walls, then loses heat through the door, the vent, and every peek. Bake elements typically draw 2,000–3,500 W while they're on.
- A slow cooker heats only a ceramic crock and a lid sitting on the food, with a small element, usually around 150–300 W. Once it's hot, it cycles.
- An electric pressure cooker uses a 700–1,000 W element, but a sealed pot under pressure cooks at about 240–250°F, so the dish finishes in a fraction of the time.
- A stovetop pot depends on the burner and the lid. A lidded Dutch oven on a low gas or induction setting can be cheap; an uncovered pot on a big electric coil is not.

## What each one uses per hour

| Electric coil or smooth-top burner, low simmer | 1,200–2,000 W element, cycling | ~0.3–0.6 kWh |

One thing that surprises people with gas ovens: many use a glow-bar igniter that draws a few hundred watts of electricity whenever the bake burner is lit. A gas oven braise uses both gas and some electricity.

Two things stand out. Long cooks are where the gap grows: a 24-hour slow-cooker broth can cost several times what a 2-hour pressure-cooker broth does. And rice is cheap any way you make it, so don't buy a gadget to save on rice.

Put it per serving and the numbers shrink further. A slow-cooker chili that costs 25 cents to cook and feeds six is about 4 cents a bowl, while the ingredients are likely $1.50–$3 a bowl.

## Four example households (illustrative)

All examples assume 52 weeks a year and use the per-batch ranges above. Swap in your own rate and cooking habits.

### 1. The Sunday oven braiser

Two people, electric oven, $0.17/kWh. A Sunday pot roast (~3 kWh) and a midweek oven chili (~2 kWh) every week.

- Now: ~5 kWh a week, ~260 kWh a year, about $44 a year.
- Both in a slow cooker: ~2.7 kWh a week, ~140 kWh a year, about $24 a year.
- Saves roughly $15–25 a year at $0.17/kWh, or about $30–40 at $0.30/kWh. A $60 slow cooker takes two to four years to pay back on energy alone, a bit faster if it also keeps the kitchen cool in summer.

### 2. The busy Instant Pot family

Family of four, electric range, $0.17/kWh. Each week: two batches of beans or stew, two pots of rice, and one roast.

- On the stovetop and in the oven: about 2 kWh for the beans and stew, 0.5 kWh for rice, and 3 kWh for the roast. ~5.5 kWh a week, ~286 kWh a year, about $49.
- All five in the Instant Pot: ~1.4 kWh a week, ~75 kWh a year, about $13.
- Saves roughly $35 a year at $0.17/kWh, or about $60 at $0.30/kWh. An $80 Instant Pot pays back in about one and a half to two and a half years. This is the household where the appliance actually earns its spot.

### 3. The gas-stove Dutch oven cook

Gas range, $1.50/therm and $0.17/kWh. Two lidded Dutch-oven braises a week on a low burner.

- Now: ~0.08 therm a week, ~4 therms a year, about $6 a year.
- The same two meals in a slow cooker: ~3 kWh a week, ~156 kWh a year, about $27 a year.
- Switching would cost about $20 more a year. Keep the Dutch oven and run the hood. Buy a slow cooker only if you want to leave the house while dinner cooks, which you shouldn't do with a lit burner.

### 4. The apartment with a microwave and a slow cooker

One person, no range, $0.30/kWh. Two 4-quart slow-cooker batches a week, portioned and reheated in the microwave.

- Slow cooker: ~1 kWh per batch × 2 = ~2 kWh a week.
- Reheating 10 portions at about 2.5 minutes each: ~0.6 kWh a week.
- Total: ~2.6 kWh a week, ~135 kWh a year, about $40 a year, or roughly 8 cents a serving.
- A 3-quart mini pressure cooker would cut the batch energy to roughly 0.25 kWh each, saving about $20–25 a year. That's three years to pay back a $70 cooker, so only make the switch if speed matters to you.

Across these four, yearly cooking energy runs from about $6 to $50, and the realistic savings from switching appliances run from nothing to about $60. For comparison, sealing drafts or fixing the thermostat schedule is often worth hundreds of dollars a year. See the [priority checklist](/blog/lower-electric-bill-priority-checklist/).

## Summer: the oven also heats your kitchen

Nearly every watt-hour an appliance uses ends up as heat inside your home, and the oven vents into the kitchen. A 3 kWh oven braise releases about 10,000 BTU into the house (1 kWh ≈ 3,412 BTU). A typical central air conditioner might use roughly 0.8–1.2 kWh to pump that heat back out.

- Electric oven braise: roughly 15–20 cents extra in AC on a hot day at $0.17/kWh, on top of the 50 cents or so of oven energy.
- Slow cooker: roughly 5–10 cents extra, and the heat trickles out over hours instead of all at once.
- Pressure cooker: a cent or two, plus some steam at release. Run the hood or vent the steam toward an open window.

In winter it works the other way: oven heat slightly offsets your heating bill, so a single-pot oven braise is less wasteful in January than in July. That's why summer is the season to move braises to the slow cooker, pressure cooker, or an outdoor grill.

## Time-of-use plans: dinner lands at peak

Many time-of-use plans charge their highest rate from roughly 4 to 9 p.m., which is exactly when most dinners cook. Check the windows on your own bill.

- A 3 kWh oven braise from 4 to 7 p.m. at a $0.40 peak rate costs about $1.20. The same roast in a slow cooker started at 8 a.m. (often off-peak or mid-peak) costs about 20–30 cents.
- An Instant Pot used at peak is still cheap: ~0.4 kWh × $0.40 is about 16 cents.
- A programmable slow cooker that switches to Warm when it's done lets you do most of the cooking before peak starts.
- Don't use a plug-in timer to delay the start of a slow cooker with raw meat in it. The food would sit at room temperature for hours before it starts heating.

## What to do first

1. Find your real rate. Electric bill total ÷ kWh used, and gas bill total ÷ therms, if you have gas.
2. Meter one cook. Plug your slow cooker or Instant Pot into a [Kill A Watt](/blog/kill-a-watt-plug-in-energy-meter/), reset it, cook your usual recipe, and read the total kWh at the end, including the Warm time you normally use.
3. Estimate the oven. Most ovens are 240 V or hardwired, so a plug meter won't fit. Look at your utility's hourly smart-meter data for an hour you cooked and nothing else big was running, or use a circuit monitor like [Emporia or Sense](/blog/emporia-vue-vs-sense/).
4. Multiply by how often you cook. kWh per batch × batches per month × your rate = what this habit really costs.
5. Compare it to your big loads. If it's a dollar or two a month, your effort is better spent on heating, cooling, and hot water first.

## Easy habits that beat buying anything

- Batch the oven. If it's on, fill it: the stew pot, a tray of roasted vegetables, and tomorrow's lunch together.
- Skip long preheats for braises, and don't open the door to check. Each peek dumps heat.
- Keep the slow cooker lid on. Many manufacturers suggest adding about 15–30 minutes of cook time per peek.
- Turn Warm off once you're eating, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.
- Use a lid on the stovetop and the lowest flame or setting that holds a simmer.
- Fill a slow cooker about half to two-thirds full. A big, nearly empty crock overcooks; an overfilled one heats too slowly.
- Use what you own. A stovetop pressure cooker or heavy lidded Dutch oven already gets you most of the savings.
- For one or two small portions, reheat in the microwave instead of the oven.

## What not to do

- Don't run the oven for one pot when a slow cooker, pressure cooker, or lidded stovetop pot would do.
- Don't buy a new appliance to save a few dollars a year if the one you have works.
- Don't leave food on Warm all evening "because it's cheap." It adds kWh and ruins texture.
- Don't put frozen meat in a slow cooker or start one on a delay timer with raw meat inside.
- Don't leave a gas burner unattended while you leave the house.
- Don't stack a kettle, toaster, air fryer, and Instant Pot on one kitchen circuit at the same time.

## Food safety

- Thaw meat and poultry in the refrigerator before slow cooking (USDA guidance). Frozen chunks can linger in the 40–140°F danger zone.
- Boil dried red kidney beans hard for about 10 minutes before slow cooking, or use canned beans. Slow-cooker temperatures may not destroy their natural toxin.
- Check doneness with a food thermometer: 165°F for poultry, 145°F plus a rest for whole cuts of beef and pork, 160°F for ground meat.
- Hold cooked food at 140°F or above, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.
- Pressure cookers: follow the manual's minimum liquid, max-fill line, and release instructions, and never force the lid open.

## Outlets, cords, and circuits

- Plug slow cookers and pressure cookers directly into a wall outlet, not an extension cord or power strip. Manufacturers' manuals say the same.
- A 6-quart Instant Pot draws about 1,000 W, roughly 8–9 amps at 120 V. A slow cooker is usually 1–2.5 amps.
- Newer US kitchens usually have at least two 20-amp countertop circuits; older kitchens may have a single 15-amp circuit. An Instant Pot plus a 1,500 W kettle on the same circuit is about 21 amps, enough to trip either one.
- Use GFCI-protected countertop outlets. If a plug or outlet feels warm or discolored, stop using it and have it checked.
- Keep cords and the appliance away from counter edges, the sink, and the stovetop.
- Don't put a cold stoneware crock onto a hot base or into a hot oven. Sudden temperature changes can crack it.

## When it isn't working right

| Problem | Likely cause | What to try |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Slow cooker food isn't hot or simmering after 4–6 hours on low | Overfilled crock, cold or frozen ingredients, lid lifted often, weak older heating element | Many extension services suggest a water test: fill half to two-thirds with water, heat on low for 8 hours, and check that it reaches about 185°F. | Burnt edges or a scorched bottom | Too little liquid, a crock too big for the batch, sugary sauce, newer cookers that run hotter | Fill half to two-thirds, use low, add sauces later, or use a smaller crock |

## Which one should you buy?

- You braise in the oven weekly and own neither: a basic 6–7 qt slow cooker is the cheapest way in. A programmable one that switches to Warm helps if you work long days.
- You want weeknight speed (beans, stock, rice, and stews in about an hour): an electric pressure cooker with a slow-cook mode replaces both. Its slow-cook mode heats from the bottom only, and some people find it runs a little cooler than a dedicated slow cooker, so allow extra time.
- You cook for one or two: a 3-quart mini pressure cooker takes less counter space and heats less mass.
- You already own a Dutch oven and cook on gas or induction: you probably don't need anything new.
- You aren't sure what your cooking costs: start with a plug-in meter and measure the cooker you already have.

## What this won't fix

- Cooking is a small slice of most bills. Space heating, cooling, and water heating are usually far larger.
- The ranges here are rough. Wattage, batch size, starting temperature, and how much the element cycles move them a lot. Your meter reading wins.
- Gas vs. electric depends on your rates. We don't declare a winner without your bill.
- Electric pressure cookers save the most time, not necessarily the most money, if you already cook on gas.

A pressure cooker usually uses the least electricity per pot, a slow cooker is close behind, a lidded pot on a gas flame is often cheapest of all, and a single-pot oven braise costs the most.
