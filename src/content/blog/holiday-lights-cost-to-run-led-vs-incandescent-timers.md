Holiday lights are one of the few seasonal loads you control completely: you pick the bulbs, the count, and the hours. The bill is just watts × hours × your rate, so the two levers are lower-watt lights and fewer hours on. Work out what your own display costs first. A timer fixes the lights-on-all-night problem for free, and you only need to replace strings where the math says it pays.


## The short version

- **One LED mini string is pocket change** — about a quarter a season on a timer at $0.17/kWh.
- **The money is in three places:** incandescent C7/C9 strings, blower inflatables, and hours (all night or 24/7).
- **Cheapest wins, in order:** read the watts → put everything on an outdoor timer with a fixed off time → unplug inflatables early → replace incandescent C9s and big incandescent runs first → leave working LED alone.
- **Watch out for dusk-to-dawn sensors without a countdown.** They run your lights all night, roughly 14–15 hours in December.

## The formula

**kWh = watts × hours on ÷ 1,000.** Cost = kWh × your all-in rate. Total watts is on the string's tag or box; add every string, inflatable, projector, and animated figure.

**Find your real rate:** total electric bill ÷ total kWh used, including delivery and fees — not just the supply price. Many US households land somewhere around $0.12–$0.30/kWh. If you're on time-of-use, write down your evening peak price separately; holiday lights run right in that window (see below).

Two things jump out. An incandescent C9 string can draw as much as 25–35 LED mini strings. And inflatables are mostly a fan — LED bulbs inside don't make the fan cheaper.

## Hours matter as much as watts

| Schedule | Hours per night | Hours over a 40-night season |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dusk to 10 pm (December) | ~5 | ~200 |
| Dusk to 11 pm | ~6 | ~240 |
| Dusk to dawn (plain photocell) | ~14–15 | ~560–600 |
| Left on 24/7 | 24 | 960 |

Dusk comes around 4:30–5:00 pm in much of the continental US in December, so "dusk to 11" is about 6 hours. A plain dusk-to-dawn sensor more than doubles that, and 24/7 quadruples it. Same lights, 2–4× the bill.

## Four worked displays (example numbers)

These are illustrative households using the typical watts above. Swap in your own tags and rate.

### A. Apartment balcony: three LED mini strings

- **Load:** 3 × ~6 W = 18 W.
- **On a 6-hour timer for 40 nights:** 18 W × 240 hr = 4.3 kWh ≈ $0.73 at $0.17.
- **Left on 24/7:** 17.3 kWh ≈ $2.94.
- **Verdict:** a timer saves about $2 a season. Buy one for convenience if you like; it's not a money project. Spend energy on [drafts and heating](/start-here/) instead.

### B. Classic incandescent roofline: four C9 strings plus six incandescent minis

- **Load:** 4 × ~175 W + 6 × ~40 W = 940 W (about 7.8 amps — fine on a 15 A circuit, but check each string's max-connect).
- **Dusk to dawn (~14 hr × 40 nights = 560 hr):** 526 kWh ≈ $89 at $0.17.
- **Same lights on a 6-hour timer (240 hr):** 226 kWh ≈ $38. The timer alone saves about $51 this season.
- **Same display in LED (4 × ~20 W C9 + 6 × ~6 W mini = ~116 W) on the timer:** 27.8 kWh ≈ $4.73 — about $34 more saved per season.
- **Verdict:** timer first (it pays back in days), then replace the C9s first because they're 700 of the 940 watts. Payback on LED replacements = what you spend ÷ about $34 per season; for example, $60 of replacement strings would pay back in roughly two seasons at this rate and schedule.

### C. Inflatable yard: three blowers, two projectors, four LED strings

- **Load:** inflatables ~100 + 150 + 200 W, projectors 2 × ~10 W, LED strings 4 × ~6 W = 494 W.
- **Left on 24/7 for 35 days (840 hr):** 415 kWh ≈ $70.50 at $0.17.
- **On a 5-hour evening timer (175 hr):** 86 kWh ≈ $14.70. Savings ≈ $56 a season.
- **Verdict:** the inflatables are 91% of the watts. LED swaps barely matter here; the timer is the whole game. Deflated inflatables also hold up better in wind and snow.

### D. Big mixed LED display in a high-rate area ($0.30/kWh)

- **Load:** 30 LED minis (~180 W) + 10 LED C9s (~200 W) + four inflatables (~480 W) + misc (~40 W) = ~900 W.
- **Dusk to about 11:30 pm, 45 nights (~292 hr):** 263 kWh ≈ $79.
- **Change:** put the inflatables on their own timer that ends 2.5 hours earlier. That's 480 W × 112 hr ≈ 54 kWh ≈ $16 saved, with the light show unchanged.
- **Verdict:** in high-rate areas, split the display into two timers — strings late, inflatables early — and consider trimming the start time if your evening peak rate applies.

## Check your home before you buy

1. **Read the tags.** Every UL- or ETL-listed string lists watts or amps (watts ≈ amps × 120). Write down each item.
2. **Meter what you can't read** — indoors, before you hang it. Plug a string, figure, or inflatable into a [Kill A Watt](/blog/kill-a-watt-plug-in-energy-meter/) for an hour and read watts or kWh. Plug-in meters are indoor devices with a ~15 A limit; don't leave one outside.
3. **Count your hours.** Dusk to 11 pm is about 6 hours in December; dusk to dawn is about 14–15; "always on" is 24.
4. **Add it up** with the cost-per-100-watts table above.
5. **Check after a week.** If your utility shows daily or hourly usage, compare a week with the display to a similar week without it. The difference should roughly match your math; if it's much higher, something is on longer than you think.

## Timer types compared

| Type | How it schedules | Good for | Watch-outs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Outdoor 24-hour mechanical dial | Push pins in 30-minute segments, repeats daily | Cheap, simple, no Wi-Fi | Drifts after a power outage until reset; no sunset tracking |
| Outdoor 7-day digital | Programmed on/off times, often several programs | Weekend vs weekday schedules | Fixed on time doesn't follow earlier sunsets; more buttons |
| Photocell dusk-to-dawn only | On at dark, off at light | Security lighting | Runs all night — not a savings device for décor |
| Photocell + countdown | On at dusk, off after 2–8 hours | Holiday displays; follows sunset automatically | Porch lights or headlights hitting the sensor can confuse it |
| Outdoor smart plug | App schedules, often sunset-based starts | Remote control, separate outlets, vacation mode | Needs Wi-Fi reaching outside; app setup |

For most displays, photocell + countdown or an outdoor smart plug with a sunset start and fixed off time gives the best mix of "on when it's dark" and "off when nobody is looking."

**Split the display.** If you have inflatables plus strings, two timers (or a two-outlet smart plug controlled separately) lets you run the fans for fewer hours than the lights, as in display D.

## Time-of-use: holiday lights run during peak

Many time-of-use plans charge the most in the late afternoon and early evening — often until about 8 or 9 pm — exactly when holiday lights are on. Check your plan's exact windows on your bill or utility account.

- **Example (illustrative):** 500 W running 4 peak hours a night for 40 nights = 80 kWh. At a $0.45 peak rate that's $36; at a $0.15 off-peak rate it would be $12.
- **What helps:** LED (fewer watts in the expensive hours), starting 30–60 minutes later, and keeping heavy items like inflatables off during peak or limited to a short window.
- **What doesn't:** running the display late at night to "use off-peak power" — nobody sees it, so it's still wasted.

## Free and cheap fixes, in order

1. **Schedule it with a fixed off time.** Dusk-to-bedtime is the biggest single cut and works with lights you already own. Outdoor-rated timers or outdoor smart plugs only.
2. **Shorten inflatable hours** — their fans run the whole time they're plugged in.
3. **Turn indoor tree and window lights off at bedtime too.** Ten incandescent window candles at ~5 W left on all night is ~50 W × 14 hr — small, but free to fix with a plug-in timer or LED candles.
4. **Replace only the heavy hitters.** Swap incandescent C7/C9 strings and big incandescent runs first; leave working LED strings alone.
5. **Don't buy more than you'll run.** The cheapest watt is the string you skip.
6. **Unplug and store** timers, smart plugs and extension cords after the season, both to stop small standby draw and to keep them out of the weather.

## Circuit, outlet and plug limits

- **String max-connect:** printed on the tag. Incandescent minis often allow only a few strings end-to-end; LEDs usually allow many more. Don't exceed it — that's how plugs and fuses overheat.
- **Circuit:** a 15 A household circuit is about 1,800 W total. For loads that run 3+ hours, staying under about 80% (≈12 A or ≈1,440 W) is good practice — and count whatever else is on that circuit (garage freezer, porch light, outdoor outlets often share).
- **Timer and plug rating:** many outdoor plug-in timers are rated around 15 A / 1,875 W, and some smart plugs less per outlet — check the label, and remember a two-outlet timer's rating is usually the total, not per outlet.
- **Cords:** use outdoor-rated extension cords sized for the load; long runs of thin cord to heavy incandescent displays get warm.

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Timer clicks on but nothing lights | GFCI outlet tripped (often moisture) | Reset the GFCI; lift connections off the ground, add drip loops, cover plug joints with outdoor-rated covers |
| GFCI trips every time it rains | Water in a connection or a damaged cord | Find it by plugging in sections one at a time; replace damaged strings; never bypass the GFCI |
| Photocell timer flickers on and off at dusk | Porch light, streetlight or headlights hitting the sensor | Aim the sensor away from other lights, or switch to a fixed schedule |
| Lights on during the day after a storm | Mechanical timer lost time in a power outage | Reset the dial; consider a digital or smart timer that keeps time |
| Smart plug offline outside | Wi-Fi doesn't reach that outlet | Move the router or add an extender, or use a non-Wi-Fi timer there |
| Bill jumped more than your math | Inflatables or strings running 24/7, or someone overriding the timer | Meter one night; check the timer's actual on/off times; check daily usage in your utility account |
| Half an incandescent mini string is dark | One loose or failed bulb in that section | Reseat or replace bulbs; if it's old, it may be time for an LED string |

## Ten-minute season plan (worksheet)

Copy this into a note before you hang anything:

1. **List every item** and its tag watts (or metered watts). Total them.
2. **Split into two groups:** "lights" (strings, projectors, candles) and "fans and motors" (inflatables, animated figures).
3. **Pick hours for each group.** For example, lights dusk to 10:30 pm, fans dusk to 9 pm.
4. **Estimate the season:** watts ÷ 100 × the cost-per-100-watts number for each group's schedule, then add them.
5. **Check the circuit:** total watts on each outdoor circuit under ~1,440 W for long runs, and each string within its max-connect.
6. **Decide on purchases last:** a timer if you don't have one; LED replacements only for the items with the biggest per-string savings in the payback table.

Most households finish this and find the answer is "one or two timers, maybe new C9s" — not a full LED replacement.

## When buying makes sense

- **You have no timer and run lights 10+ hours a night:** an outdoor timer or outdoor smart plug usually pays back in one season on any display bigger than a couple of strings.
- **You have inflatables:** a timer that ends their night early is the highest-return purchase in this guide (display C).
- **You run incandescent C9s or lots of incandescent minis all night:** LED replacements usually pay back in one to three seasons depending on hours and rate.
- **You're on a high rate or time-of-use plan:** the same swaps pay back roughly twice as fast at $0.30 as at $0.17.
- **You already run LED on a schedule:** you're done. Spend the effort on [heating and drafts](/start-here/) instead, where the dollars are much larger.

## Safety rules

- No indoor plugs, timers, or extension cords outdoors. Use outdoor-rated gear on GFCI outlets.
- Don't exceed the max-connect number printed on a string, or the plug's or timer's watt/amp rating.
- Don't staple or nail through wires; use plastic clips. Keep connections off the ground and out of snow and puddles.
- Throw out strings with cracked insulation or broken sockets. Don't run cords through closed doors or windows where they get pinched.
- Keep inflatables staked and tethered; unplug them in high wind.
- Turn displays off when you're away or asleep if they aren't on a timer.

## What this won't fix

Holiday lighting is a small, seasonal part of most bills — even the big incandescent example above is under $100 for a season. Wattages above are typical, not universal; your strings' labels win. Payback numbers assume your rate, hours, and what you pay for replacements.
