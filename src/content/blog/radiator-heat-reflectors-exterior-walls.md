Radiators and electric baseboards on **exterior walls** do two jobs at once: they warm the room you live in, and they warm the cold wall behind them. That second job is mostly waste — masonry, plaster, or thin cavity walls soak radiant heat that never reaches the sofa. **Radiator heat reflectors** (foil-faced foam panels or double-bubble reflective insulation cut and placed behind the heater) are a cheap, reversible way to send more of that radiant energy back into the room. This guide is **bill-first**: measure which units sit on exterior walls, choose Reflectix-class rolls vs a dedicated panel, install with real clearances, and skip invented savings percentages.

If your heat is **electric baseboard**, also read [line-voltage thermostats](/blog/electric-baseboard-heater-thermostats-line-voltage/) — Nest/Ecobee are the wrong family. This page is about **foil/foam behind heaters on cold exterior walls**, not a furnace swap or an attic radiant-barrier project.

## Honest framing: redirect radiant heat, don’t invent R-miracles

Heat reflectors help when:

- A **hydronic or steam radiator** hangs on an exterior wall (or under a window on that wall) with a few inches of gap behind the fins.
- **Electric baseboard** or wall convectors run along an exterior wall and the plaster or drywall behind them runs noticeably warmer than the rest of the room.
- You’ve already done the cheap draft work, so redirected heat isn’t immediately leaking out the sash.
- You can cut a clean foil face that looks toward the heater without touching hot metal or blocking valves.

Reflectors do **less** when:

- The heater is on an **interior partition** between heated rooms — there’s little “cold sink” to fight.
- The room is dominated by infiltration (door undercuts, failed weatherstrip, open chimney) — fix those first.
- You expect a package **R-value** printed for attic or duct use to apply unchanged behind a radiator.
- Furniture, long drapes, or stored boxes already block the heater’s face into the room (clear the front first).
- You tape foil **over** electric elements or stuff it into a gas fireplace / combustion appliance area.

**We do not guarantee savings.** Lab or marketing claims about “up to X%” depend on wall U-factor, outdoor temperature, heater type, coverage area, and whether an air gap exists. Your rates and habits decide the bill. Treat reflectors as a **small materials upgrade** that improves comfort and reduces wall-wasted radiant heat — not as proof you can skip insulation or a working thermostat.

Also note: **Reflectix and similar reflective products advertise R-values that are application-dependent.** Behind a radiator, shop for a durable foil face + thin foam/bubble core you can cut to fit — not for attic-stack math.

## Measure-first walk: walls, gaps, and heater type (before you buy)

Do this once with the heat off or cooled enough to work safely behind the unit:

1. **List every radiator or baseboard on an exterior wall** — bedrooms facing north, living rooms under bay windows, kitchens on the cold side of the house. Skip interior-partition units for this project. Photograph each so you remember pipe locations and bracket spacing.
2. **Hydronic / steam radiator vs electric baseboard vs other** — cast-iron or panel radiators usually have a clear gap to the wall; electric baseboard often sits tighter with a factory back panel. Reflectors still help on exterior-wall electric runs when you can slide or mount a strip **behind** the chassis without touching elements. Fan-forced wall heaters and open-coil units need stricter clearance reading — when unsure, skip DIY foil and call an electrician or HVAC tech.
3. **Confirm it is an exterior wall** — knock/test: exterior walls are colder in winter, often thicker, and sit under eaves or against brick. If both sides of the wall are heated rooms, a reflector is low priority.
4. **Measure the gap behind the heater** — note depth from wall to fins/chassis, width of the unit, and height you can cover without blocking valves, bleeders, TRVs, electrical junction covers, or airflow slots. Typical DIY targets cover the wall area **shadowed by the heater**, not the whole room.
5. **Measure width × height for material math** — for Reflectix, a 24″-wide roll is the common US size; run it horizontally or vertically and seam with foil tape. For a dedicated ~100×70 cm panel, confirm one sheet covers one radiator or plan to butt two. Add 10–15% for mistakes and cutouts around brackets.
6. **Check heat source and clearances** — electric: kill the correct breaker before removing covers; read the heater’s clearance labels. Hydronic/steam: cool the system or isolate the zone if you can; protect finished floors from drips when you move the unit slightly. Never block combustion air for boilers, furnaces, or water heaters while “sealing” a mechanical room.
7. **Decide roll vs dedicated panel vs seal-first instead** — multiple units or long baseboard → Reflectix 25′ roll. One showcase radiator and you want a pre-sized foam+foil sheet → dedicated panel. Still have screaming door drafts → [weatherstripping](/blog/best-weatherstripping-draft-stoppers/) and [window film](/blog/window-insulation-kits-winter-drafts/) before Amazon.
8. **Renters and adhesive rules** — check the lease. Prefer tuck-behind, light removable tape, or painter’s-tape test patches over aggressive construction adhesive on landlord paint. Owners: foil HVAC tape on seams is fine; avoid cloth duct tape that dries brittle.

If electric baseboard comfort is terrible because the **thermostat** chatters or never sets back, fix controls with a proper [line-voltage thermostat](/blog/electric-baseboard-heater-thermostats-line-voltage/) — a reflector does not replace a wrong Nest on 240V wiring.

## Situation priorities + do-not list

### Prioritize radiator / baseboard reflectors when

1. **Exterior-wall radiators or baseboards** are your main room heat and the wall behind them runs hot to the touch while the room still feels chilly.
2. **Old solid masonry or poorly insulated cavity walls** sit directly behind the heater (common in pre-1980 US housing and many brick rows).
3. **You’ve already sealed the obvious drafts** and still want a cheap next step before bigger insulation work.
4. **Several units need coverage** — one Reflectix BP24025-class roll often beats buying many small imported panels.
5. **Electric resistance heat** is expensive per kWh in your rate plan — every bit of heat kept in the room helps, alongside setbacks and sealing.

### Do-not list (safety + wasted money)

- **Do not cover glowing electric elements, open coils, or fan outlets with foil or foam.**
- **Do not block radiator valves, bleeders, TRVs, pipe unions, or electrical covers** — leave service access.
- **Do not stuff reflectors into combustion appliance zones** or block boiler / furnace make-up air.
- **Do not treat marketed Reflectix R-values as guaranteed wall R-adders** behind a radiator.
- **Do not skip draft sealing** and expect foil alone to fix a leaky room.
- **Do not buy sketchy no-name “Reflectix-named” marketplace rolls** with week-long overseas shipping when a verified US listing exists.
- **Do not use cloth duct tape as the long-term seam** — use foil HVAC tape (Nashua 322 class).
- **Do not invent dollar or percent savings** for a social post — climate and walls decide outcomes.
- **Renters: do not permanently glue** panels without permission.

## Honest limits (what a radiator reflector will not fix)

- A wide-open envelope — door gaps, single-pane wash, attic bypasses ([checklist](/blog/lower-electric-bill-priority-checklist/)).
- Wrong thermostat family on electric baseboard ([line-voltage guide](/blog/electric-baseboard-heater-thermostats-line-voltage/)).
- Guaranteed R-value or bill cuts from a package claim.
- Heat blocked by furniture or floor-length drapes piled on the heater.
- A failing boiler, air-bound radiator, or dead electric element.
- Combustion safety or carbon-monoxide problems — detectors and pro service, not foil.
- Whole-wall insulation upgrades you still need for a gut remodel — this is a behind-heater patch, not cavity foam.

## Post-setup checklist + habits

1. Confirm every covered heater is on an **exterior** wall you intended to treat.
2. Recheck **clearances** on electric units after the first full heat cycle (warm panel OK; hot-to-scorching contact not OK).
3. Open/close radiator valves and bleeders once to prove you didn’t bury them.
4. Keep **3 ft** clearance in front of electric baseboards; don’t drape clothes on radiators.
5. Pair with nightly [thermal curtains](/blog/thermal-blackout-curtains-energy-savings/) on the same cold glass — open south glass on sunny winter days when it helps.
6. Log comfort for a week at the same thermostat setting — not as a guaranteed savings proof, as a reality check.
7. Store leftover Reflectix flat or loosely rolled for the next cold room; keep foil tape in the tote.
8. Schedule pro help for inaccessible steam piping, aluminum wiring quirks, or any heater that smells wrong after install.

### Habits that multiply the upgrade

- Seal first, reflect second, then set back setpoints you can live with.
- Dust foil faces at the start of each heating season — dirty foil reflects less.
- Don’t run portable heaters against the same wall foil as a “more is more” stack on one circuit.
- On mild days, lower setpoints; let the reflector reduce wall loss when the system does run.
- When you renovate, ask about exterior continuous insulation — behind-radiator foil is a bridge, not the destination.

## FAQ

### Do radiator heat reflectors actually lower my heating bill?

They can help when a radiator or electric baseboard sits on a cold exterior wall and a lot of radiant heat is absorbed by that wall instead of the room. The foil face reflects radiant energy back inward; a foam or bubble layer adds a thin conductive break. Results depend on wall construction, how cold it is outside, how much of the heater faces the wall, and whether you already sealed drafts. We do not guarantee a percentage or dollar cut — treat this as a small, cheap comfort and waste-reduction upgrade after weatherization, not a furnace replacement.

### Reflectix roll or a dedicated radiator reflector panel — which should I buy?

Most US DIY homes with several exterior-wall radiators or long baseboard runs do better with a verified Reflectix double-bubble roll (24″ × 25′ primary, or 24″ × 10′ for a small job) cut to fit behind each unit. Dedicated foam+foil panels (tesamoll-class ~100×70 cm) are purpose-shaped for classic wall radiators but cost more per square foot and often need separate adhesive. Buy the dedicated panel when you want one neat sheet behind a single European-style radiator and you accept the higher $/area; buy Reflectix when you need coverage for multiple units or electric baseboard lengths.

### Can I put Reflectix behind electric baseboard heaters?

Often yes on the wall behind the heater — not draped over the fins or touching glowing elements. Keep manufacturer clearances, leave service access, and never stuff foil against open coils or fan outlets. Pair with the right line-voltage thermostat if controls are the real problem (Nest/Ecobee are 24V — wrong family). If the baseboard is on an interior partition, reflectors do little; prioritize exterior-wall units.

### Will Reflectix give me the R-value printed on the package behind a radiator?

Not in a simple “add R-whatever to the wall” way. Manufacturer and marketing R-values for reflective insulation are highly application-dependent (air gaps, orientation, whether the foil faces a sealed cavity). Behind a radiator you mainly want a clean reflective face toward the heater plus a thin foam/bubble spacer so the wall stops soaking radiant heat. Do not shop by a headline R-number alone, and do not invent attic-style performance for a cut panel taped to drywall.

### Is it safe to install radiator reflector foil myself?

Usually, if you cut power or cool hydronic systems first, keep material off hot elements, maintain clearances listed for electric heaters, and do not block combustion air, valves, or bleeders. Use foil HVAC tape for seams — not cloth duct tape that dries out. Renters should check the lease before adhesive on painted walls; removable tape or tuck-behind installs without glue are kinder. Hire a pro if you cannot safely access behind steam/hydronic piping or if electric clearances are unclear.

### Should I seal drafts before buying radiator reflectors?

Yes. Reflectors do not fix door gaps, leaky sash, or an open attic stair. Do weatherstripping, window film, and obvious air sealing first so the heat you redirect into the room actually stays there. Then add reflectors on exterior-wall radiators or baseboards that still feel like they cook the plaster more than the air.
