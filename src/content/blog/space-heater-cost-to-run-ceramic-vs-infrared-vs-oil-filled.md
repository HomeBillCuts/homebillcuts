Every winter, the same question shows up: *which space heater is cheapest to run?* The honest answer is a little disappointing and very useful: **at the same wattage, they all cost the same.** A 1,500 W ceramic fan heater, a 1,500 W quartz infrared heater, and a 1,500 W oil-filled radiator each turn about 1.5 kWh of electricity into about 1.5 kWh of heat for every hour they run at full power. What you're really choosing is **how the heat reaches you** — moving warm air, radiant warmth, or slow, steady heat — plus noise, safety, and cycling. This guide is **bill-first**: do the cost math with your rate, measure with a plug-in meter, check whether a space heater saves or costs money in *your* house, then pick the type. No guaranteed savings.

## The one fact the ads leave out: ~100% efficient, all of them

Plug-in electric space heaters are **resistance heaters**. Electricity runs through a heating element (a ceramic PTC stone, a quartz tube, a coiled wire, or a wire inside sealed oil) and becomes heat. Practically all of the energy ends up as heat in your room — even the bit that runs the fan and display. That's why resistance heat is called "~100% efficient" at the point of use — and why it's still often expensive next to a heat pump.

That means:

- **Watts decide the cost.** A 1,500 W heater uses 1.5 kWh per hour at full power no matter what's inside it. A 750 W setting uses 0.75 kWh per hour.
- **Heat output is fixed by watts too.** 1 W ≈ 3.412 Btu/h, so a 1,500 W heater makes about **5,100 Btu per hour**. A listing that promises "60% more heat" from 1,500 W is describing distribution, not more energy.
- **Thermal mass doesn't create free heat.** An oil-filled radiator keeps radiating after it clicks off because it stored heat while it was on. You paid for that heat already. It smooths the temperature swing; it doesn't cut kWh.
- **"ECO mode" isn't magic either.** ECO, ComfortTemp, and similar modes lower the wattage or cycle the element once the room reaches the setpoint. That can reduce overshoot — a real, small benefit — but a kWh is still a kWh.

**What does change between types?** How fast you *feel* warm, whether it heats the air or you, cycling, noise, surface heat, and tip risk. Those can change **how many hours you run it and at what setting** — the only way type affects your bill.

## Cost math: watts × hours × your rate

The formula is the same for every heater:

**Cost = (watts ÷ 1,000) × hours × your rate per kWh**

**Find your rate first.** Take the **total electric charges** on a recent bill (energy, delivery, and riders — not just the "energy charge" line) and divide by the **kWh used** that month. That all-in number is what an extra kWh actually costs you. For context, the U.S. Energy Information Administration's preliminary figure for the average U.S. residential price was **18.31¢ per kWh in July 2026** ([EIA Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.3](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/epm_table_grapher.php?t=epmt_5_3)) — but state averages range from the low teens to well over 30¢, and your utility's winter or tiered rates may be different again.

### Worked examples (example rates — swap in your bill's rate)

All numbers below use an **example rate of $0.18/kWh** (rounded from the EIA national average above) unless labeled otherwise. They're arithmetic illustrations, not predictions of your bill.

| Scenario | kWh | Cost at $0.18/kWh |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1,500 W on High, 1 hour, element on the whole time | 1.5 | **$0.27** |
| 750 W on Low, 1 hour | 0.75 | **~$0.14** |
| 1,500 W, 8-hour night, never cycles off (drafty room or no thermostat) | 12.0 | **$2.16** |
| 1,500 W, 8-hour night, thermostat holds the room so the element is on ~50% of the time | 6.0 | **$1.08** |
| 750 W, 4-hour evening, element on the whole time | 3.0 | **$0.54** |
| 1,500 W ceramic at a desk, 2 hours/day on High, 22 workdays | 66.0 | **$11.88/month** |
| 1,500 W, 8 h/night at ~50% duty, 30 nights | 180 | **$32.40/month** |

**Other example rates:** at $0.12/kWh, 1,500 W costs $0.18 an hour; at $0.30/kWh, $0.45 an hour. Scale any row above by your rate ÷ 0.18.

The 50% duty cycle is invented to show a thermostat's effect; real on-time depends on room size, insulation, drafts, weather, and setpoint. That's why you measure.

### Measure it with a Kill A Watt (10 minutes of setup, one real night)

1. **Read the label** on the heater (bottom or back). Most US portable heaters max out at **1,500 W (~12.5 A at 120 V)**; many have a 600–900 W Low.
2. **Plug a [Kill A Watt](/blog/kill-a-watt-plug-in-energy-meter/) directly into the wall** and the heater directly into the meter. No power strip or extension cord in the chain. If the meter or plug gets hot, stop.
3. **Run a normal evening or night** the way you'd actually use it — same setting, same door open or closed.
4. **Read kWh and elapsed time** in the morning. kWh ÷ hours = your real average draw. Multiply kWh by your all-in rate for the night's cost.
5. **Repeat on a similar-weather night** after you change something (lower setting, door closed, draft sealed, a [plug-in thermostat](/blog/plug-in-thermostats-space-heaters/), or a different heater) and compare kWh per hour.

A [whole-home monitor](/blog/emporia-vue-vs-sense/) or your utility's hourly smart-meter data also shows whether the furnace or heat pump actually ran less that night.

## When a space heater saves money — and when it costs more

The heater's own cost is only half the story. The other half is **what your central heat stops doing** while the space heater runs.

### It can save when you really zone-heat

A space heater lowers the bill only if you **turn the whole-house thermostat down** and heat just the room you're in, with the door closed. The DOE says turning your thermostat back 7–10°F for 8 hours a day can save as much as 10% a year on heating ([DOE](https://www.energy.gov/indianenergy/articles/six-simple-steps-save-energy-and-money-winter)) — that's the central-heat savings a space heater can make livable. It works best when:

- **Your central heat is expensive per unit of heat:** an electric furnace or baseboard (same cost per kWh as a space heater, so heating one room instead of the whole house is a straightforward win), propane, or heating oil.
- **You live in one or two rooms** for long stretches — a home office, a bedroom at night, a den in the evening.
- **The room holds heat:** the door closes and obvious drafts are sealed ([weatherstripping](/blog/best-weatherstripping-draft-stoppers/), [window kits](/blog/window-insulation-kits-winter-drafts/), [outlet gaskets](/blog/air-sealing-caulk-foam-outlet-gaskets/)).

### It often costs more in a heat-pump home

A heat pump moves heat instead of making it. DOE says heat pumps can provide **up to three times more heat than the energy they use**, and can reduce electricity use for heating by about **50% compared with electric furnaces or baseboard heaters** ([DOE Energy Saver guide, PDF](https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2022-08/energy-saver-guide-2022.pdf)). A space heater makes one unit of heat per unit of electricity.

**Example math only:** if your heat pump is running at an efficiency of about 2.5 units of heat per unit of electricity on a mild day, the 1.5 kWh of heat a space heater makes in an hour would take the heat pump about **0.6 kWh** to deliver. At $0.18/kWh, that's roughly **$0.11 of heat-pump electricity vs $0.27 of space-heater electricity** for the same warmth. One small closed room can still beat warming a big house, but it's a close call, and in mild weather the heat pump usually wins.

Two caveats: on very cold days many heat pumps lean on **backup resistance strips** (AUX or EM heat), which cost about the same per kWh as a space heater; and DOE advises **not** setting a heat pump back manually if recovery triggers that backup heat. Check your thermostat's recovery behavior before counting on a setback.

### Gas furnace: it depends on your two rates

Compare cost per unit of *delivered* heat. One therm of natural gas is 100,000 Btu, about **29.3 kWh** of heat. Divide your gas price per therm by (29.3 × the furnace's efficiency):

- **Example:** $1.50/therm in a 95% efficient furnace → $1.50 ÷ (29.3 × 0.95) ≈ **$0.054 per kWh of heat**. In an older 80% furnace → about **$0.064**.
- At **$0.18/kWh** electricity, the space heater's heat costs about **3× as much** per unit as the gas furnace's in this example.

Zone heating can still win in a gas home if one small room replaces a deep whole-house setback, but the margin is thin. Use your own gas and electric rates and the furnace's AFUE from its label.

### It always costs more when you stack heat

Leave the whole-house thermostat alone, add a 1,500 W heater, and you pay for both — the most common reason bills rise after buying one.

## Ceramic vs infrared/quartz vs oil-filled: what actually differs

| | Ceramic / fan-forced (PTC) | Infrared / quartz radiant | Oil-filled radiator |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **How you feel it** | Warm air blown at you, then the room | Radiant heat on skin and objects in its line of sight, almost instantly | Gentle, even warmth after a slow start; heats room air by convection plus some radiant |
| **Warm-up** | Fast (seconds to a minute) | Instant at close range | Slow (often 15–30+ minutes to feel the room change) |
| **Cycling** | Short on/off cycles, sometimes noticeable temperature swings | Short cycles; warmth stops quickly when it cycles off | Smooth; keeps radiating after it switches off |
| **Noise** | Fan noise (varies widely by model) | Quiet without a fan; many towers and cabinet models add one | Silent except light ticking as metal expands |
| **Surface heat** | Often cool-touch housing; hot air at the grille | Very hot, glowing elements behind a grille | Fins and body get hot to the touch |
| **Best fit** | Quick spot heat you'll turn off when you leave: desk, bathroom (only if rated), mornings | Spot heat in drafty rooms, workshops, standing at a counter or desk | Long sessions in one closed room: bedroom, office, den |
| **Watch-outs** | Tip-over risk, dust smell, dry-feeling air for some people | Combustibles in front of it; poor match for bedding, curtains, toddlers | Slow to react; can't be moved quickly; hot fins |

**A note on "infrared cabinet" heaters.** Many heavy wood-cabinet heaters sold as "infrared" (some with "copper heat exchangers") blow air over quartz or PTC elements — effectively a quiet fan heater in a furniture box, still ~5,100 Btu/h at 1,500 W.

### Can the type lower your bill indirectly?

Sometimes — through **use**, not efficiency:

- **Radiant at a desk or drafty spot:** a 750 W quartz heater aimed at your legs can replace a 1,500 W fan heater fighting a draft for the whole room.
- **Fast heat you switch off:** 20 minutes of ceramic while you dress can beat a slow oil heater left on all morning.
- **Smooth cycling:** oil-filled can overshoot less than a crude fan-heater dial — though a digital thermostat or [plug-in thermostat](/blog/plug-in-thermostats-space-heaters/) narrows the gap.
- **Fewer watts when enough:** a [heated mattress pad](/blog/heated-mattress-pads-zone-heating/) or [under-desk heater](/blog/under-desk-heaters-foot-warmers-home-office/) uses a fraction of a room heater's draw.

Measure with the Kill A Watt to know whether a change actually used fewer kWh.

| Your situation | Best-fit type | Why | Watch-out |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Quick spot heat at a desk** for an hour or two | Small ceramic, or a 750 W quartz infrared aimed at you | Fast; you feel it right away; easy to turn off | Keep paper and bags 3 ft away; consider a [90–235 W foot warmer](/blog/under-desk-heaters-foot-warmers-home-office/) first |
| **Bedroom, evening and overnight** | Oil-filled with thermostat/ECO and timer | Silent, steady, no glowing element | Hot fins; 3 ft from bedding and curtains; for sleep-only warmth a [heated mattress pad](/blog/heated-mattress-pads-zone-heating/) uses far fewer watts |
| **Drafty room you can't fully seal yet** | Infrared/quartz aimed at where you sit | Warms you, not the escaping air | Seal drafts anyway — any heater runs nonstop in a leaky room |
| **Garage workbench or basement corner** | Infrared/quartz | Spot heat without warming a huge, leaky space | Sawdust, solvents, and gas cans don't belong near any heater; follow the manual on garage use |
| **Kids or pets in the room** | Ceramic with cool-touch housing, tip-over and overheat protection, and child lock; or an oil-filled unit placed out of reach | Fewer exposed hot surfaces | Supervision still required; no glowing quartz element at toddler height; secure cords |
| **Heat-pump home in mild weather** | Usually none — let the heat pump work | Heat pump heat is often cheaper per unit | Watch for AUX heat on cold-day recovery |
| **The whole house is cold (failed furnace)** | Call for repair; use heaters only as a short, supervised stopgap | A 1,500 W heater won't heat a house | Never use gas ovens or grills indoors for heat |

## Safety do-not list (non-negotiable)

Space heaters start home fires every winter when clearance and cord rules slip. Before you plug one in:

- **Do not** use an extension cord or power strip. Plug the heater **directly into a wall outlet**. ESFI's guidance is the same: plug space heaters directly into the wall; extension cords and power strips can overheat ([ESFI](https://www.esfi.org/space-heater-safety/)).
- **Do not** put anything within **3 feet** of the heater — curtains, bedding, sofas, clothes, papers, boxes, or pet beds. Never dry clothes or towels on a heater.
- **Do not** buy a heater without **tip-over shutoff and overheat protection**.
- **Do not** buy an unlisted heater. Look for a mark from a nationally recognized testing lab — **UL, ETL (Intertek), or CSA** — on the product label, not just the listing.
- **Do not** leave a heater running **unattended**, or in a room where you're **sleeping**, unless its manual explicitly allows it and you've accepted the risk. In particular, keep ceramic and quartz heaters well away from beds and bedding.
- **Do not** share the circuit with other big loads. A 15 A, 120 V circuit tops out at **1,800 W**, and electricians size long-running loads at about 80% of that (~1,440 W). A 1,500 W heater on High should essentially have the circuit to itself — add a hair dryer, kettle, or second heater and the breaker trips, or a weak connection overheats.
- **Do not** keep using a heater whose plug, cord, or the outlet feels hot, discolors, or smells — unplug and have the outlet checked; replace a damaged heater.
- **Do not** run a cord under a rug, or set the heater on furniture or thick carpet unless the manual allows it.
- **Do not** use a heater in a bathroom, garage, or near water unless the manufacturer rates it for that location.
- **Do not** disable or bypass a tip-over switch because it trips on carpet — move the heater to a hard, level floor.
- **Do** keep working smoke alarms, and CO alarms if you have any fuel-burning appliances. Electric heaters don't make CO, but they can start fires.

## "Miracle heater" red flags (and what's really going on)

Every fall brings ads for heaters that "cut your bill in half." Physics sets hard limits on a plug-in heater: on a standard US outlet it's capped near **1,500 W ≈ 5,100 Btu/h**, and it can't make more heat than the electricity it uses. Red flags:

| Claim | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| "Cuts your heating bill 50%" | Any savings come from turning the central heat down and heating one room — any inexpensive heater does that too. Added on top, it raises the bill. |
| "Uses less electricity than other heaters" | At the same watts and hours, every resistance heater uses the same kWh. Check the wattage on the label. |
| "Heats 1,000 sq ft" | 5,100 Btu/h warms one small-to-medium, reasonably sealed room in most climates — not a whole floor. |
| "Doesn't burn oxygen / dry the air" | No electric heater burns oxygen. Warmer air holds the same moisture at lower *relative* humidity — that's true for every heater. |
| "Patented copper / nano-ceramic / Amish-built heat exchanger" | Materials change looks, noise, and surface temperature, not how much heat a watt makes. |
| Tiny plug-in "outlet heaters" that "heat a whole room" | Low-watt plug-in cubes make a few hundred watts at most. Fine for a cold corner; not room heat. |

This isn't hypothetical: in 2012 an advertising self-regulation program recommended that the marketer of one widely advertised portable heater discontinue claims about monthly savings it couldn't support ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/cro/news/2012/08/ad-watchdog-tells-edenpure-to-tone-down-space-heater-claims/index.htm)). Buy on safety features, controls, noise, and fit for the room, and let a Kill A Watt tell you what it costs.

## Troubleshooting: "my space heater bill is too high"

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bill went up after buying a heater | Central thermostat never moved (stacked heat) | Lower the whole-house setpoint on a [schedule](/blog/best-smart-thermostats-2026/) while you zone-heat; meter the heater |

| Plug or outlet feels hot | Worn receptacle, loose plug, or a strip/cord in the chain | Unplug; remove the cord/strip; have the outlet replaced |
| Heat-pump home bill rose | Resistance heat replacing cheaper heat-pump heat, or AUX heat on recovery | Let the heat pump carry the house in mild weather; check thermostat recovery settings |

## Honest limits

- **No type is more efficient than another** at the same watts. Anyone claiming otherwise is selling something.
- **A space heater is a zone tool.** It saves money only when the central heat runs less — and in heat-pump and many gas homes, the math is tight or negative.
- **A 1,500 W heater heats one room**, not a house, and can't keep up with a very drafty room.
- **Duty cycles in our examples are invented** to show the arithmetic. Your Kill A Watt reading replaces them.
- **Safety beats savings.** No bill trim is worth a fire; follow the do-not list and your manual.
- **No universal number.** Rates, climate, insulation, and habits vary too much for honest "you'll save $X" claims.

## Checklist before you buy (or before you plug in)

- [ ] All-in electric rate from your bill written down (and gas rate per therm if you have gas heat)
- [ ] Know your central heat type: electric resistance, heat pump (with AUX?), gas, propane, or oil
- [ ] One room picked, door closes, obvious drafts sealed
- [ ] Plan to lower the whole-house thermostat while the heater runs
- [ ] Heater has tip-over + overheat protection and a UL/ETL/CSA mark
- [ ] Wall outlet on a lightly loaded circuit, 3 ft clearance, no cords or strips
- [ ] Kill A Watt reading for one normal night, then again after any change

## FAQ

### Which type of space heater is cheapest to run — ceramic, infrared, or oil-filled?

At the same wattage and the same hours, none of them. Every plug-in resistance heater turns essentially all of the electricity it draws into heat, so a 1,500 W ceramic, a 1,500 W quartz infrared, and a 1,500 W oil-filled radiator each use 1.5 kWh per hour of full-power running. Type changes how the heat reaches you, noise, warm-up speed, and cycling — not efficiency. The cheapest heater is the one that keeps you comfortable at the lowest setting for the fewest hours.

### How much does it cost to run a 1,500 W space heater?

Multiply kilowatts by hours by your rate. A 1,500 W heater running flat out uses 1.5 kWh per hour, so at an example rate of $0.18 per kWh it costs about $0.27 an hour, or $2.16 for eight hours. On Low (often 750 W) it's about half that. Thermostats cycle the element off once the room warms, so real use is usually lower. Use your bill's all-in rate (total electric charges ÷ kWh) and measure a real night with a plug-in meter like a Kill A Watt.

### Does a space heater save money compared to central heat?

Only when it lets you turn the central heat down. Heating one closed room while the whole-house thermostat drops can cost less than heating every room, especially if your central heat is electric resistance, propane, or oil. Added on top of an unchanged thermostat, it's extra load. In heat-pump homes, resistance heat often costs more per unit of warmth than the heat pump, which the U.S. Department of Energy says can deliver up to three times more heat than the electricity it uses. With a gas furnace, it depends on your gas and electric rates.

### Are infrared heaters more energy efficient?

Not in the sense the ads imply. An infrared or quartz heater still turns 1,500 W into about 5,100 Btu per hour, the same as any other 1,500 W heater. It heats people and objects in its line of sight, so you feel warm quickly in a drafty room or at a desk. That can let you run it fewer hours or on Low — a usage change, not a more efficient heater. Big wooden-cabinet "infrared" heaters usually push fan-forced air over quartz or PTC elements, so they behave more like a fan heater.

### Which space heater type is safest for a bedroom or a home with kids and pets?

No type is safe without clearance and supervision, but the risks differ. Oil-filled radiators have no glowing element and are quiet for bedrooms, but their fins get hot and they're heavy to knock over. Ceramic heaters often have cool-touch housings but blow hot air and can tip. Quartz infrared heaters have very hot, visible elements behind a grille and are a poor match for bedding, curtains, and curious toddlers. Whatever you pick: tip-over and overheat protection, a UL, ETL, or CSA listing mark, plugged directly into a wall outlet, 3 feet of clearance, and never left running unattended or near bedding while you sleep.

### Do "cut your heating bill 50%" heaters really work?

Be skeptical. A plug-in heater can't produce more heat than the electricity it uses, and on a standard 120 V outlet it's limited to about 1,500 W, roughly 5,100 Btu per hour, no matter what the heat exchanger is made of. Real savings come from heating fewer rooms while you lower the central thermostat — any inexpensive heater does that. In 2012, an advertising self-regulation program recommended that one heavily advertised heater's marketer discontinue unsupported monthly-savings claims. Treat "heats 1,000 sq ft," "uses less electricity than other heaters," and "doesn't burn oxygen" as marketing, not math.

## Bottom line

Ceramic, infrared/quartz, and oil-filled space heaters all cost the same per hour at the same wattage — **watts × hours × your rate**. Pick the type for the room: **ceramic** for quick spot heat you'll switch off, **infrared/quartz** for warming yourself in a drafty spot, **oil-filled** for quiet, steady heat in one closed room. The bill only goes down when the **central heat goes down** with it, and in heat-pump homes (and often gas homes) resistance heat is the pricier heat. **Find your all-in rate → meter a real night with a Kill A Watt → lower the whole-house setpoint → wall outlet, 3 ft clearance, tip-over protection, never unattended.** Amazon links may earn a commission as an Associate ([disclosure](/disclosure/)).
