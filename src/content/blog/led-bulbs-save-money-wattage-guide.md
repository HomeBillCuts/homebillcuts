LED bulbs are one of the easiest efficiency upgrades left in many US homes — but “easy” is not the same as “will transform your electric bill.” Lighting is often a smaller slice of household kWh than HVAC or water heating. This guide covers **when LEDs actually save money**, how to walk the house and prioritize sockets, how to swap watts for lumens without buying junk, a buyer decision tree, and a short verified Amazon list — without invented savings guarantees.

## Do LED bulbs actually save money?

**Usually yes on a per-bulb basis** when you replace high-hour incandescent or halogen lamps with quality LEDs of similar light output. LEDs use far less wattage for the same lumens and last longer, so you buy fewer replacements.

**Often modest on the whole bill** if:

- You already swapped most sockets years ago.
- Remaining bulbs are in closets and other low-hour fixtures.
- HVAC dominates your usage (common in hot or cold climates).

**Still worth doing** because the upfront cost is low, the install is DIY-safe for standard screw-base lamps, and leftover heat from incandescents is waste you don’t need indoors in summer.

**Honest framing:** We do **not** claim a fixed annual dollar savings. Your rate ($/kWh), hours of use, and how many inefficient bulbs remain decide the math.

### Quick payback math (fill in your numbers)

Use this once for a high-hour socket so expectations stay honest:

1. Note **old watts** and **new watts** from the packages (or meter a fixture cluster if you have a plug-in meter).
2. Estimate **hours per day** the light is actually on.
3. Rough yearly kWh saved: `(old W − new W) × hours/day × 365 ÷ 1000`.
4. Multiply by **your** $/kWh from the bill — not a national average graphic.

Example shape only: a 60W lamp replaced by a 9W LED at 4 hours/day is about `51 × 4 × 365 ÷ 1000 ≈ 74 kWh/year`. At $0.16/kWh that’s roughly ~$12/year for *that one socket* — fine, but not a reason to ignore a stuck heat-pump aux strip or a drafty attic hatch.

## Measure first: walk the house before you buy a 24-pack

Gadgets before a 10-minute walk is how you end up with a closet full of 5000K garage bulbs. Do this once:

1. **List high-hour rooms** — kitchen, living, main bath, home office, porch left on for safety, garage shop.
2. **Note what’s still inefficient** — incandescent, halogen torchieres, old CFLs you hate, failing LEDs that flicker.
3. **Count form factors** — A19 medium base, BR30 cans, candelabra, GU10/MR16, outdoor wet-rated globes. Don’t buy one multipack shape for every hole.
4. **Check dimmers and enclosed fixtures** before you open Amazon — dimmable label and enclosure rating matter more than brand storytelling.
5. **Buy for the top sockets first** — finish the rooms that burn hours every day; leave guest closets for a leftover pack later.

If you want proof lighting is (or isn’t) a big slice of kWh, a [whole-home monitor](/blog/emporia-vue-vs-sense/) or a cheap [plug-in meter](/blog/kill-a-watt-plug-in-energy-meter/) on a lamp cluster beats guessing. After high-hour lamps are done, hunt always-on electronics with [smart plugs for vampire power](/blog/best-smart-plugs-vampire-power/).

## Wattage swap guide (think lumens, not “60W LED”)

Incandescent wattage was a rough proxy for brightness. With LEDs, shop **lumens** (light output) and **CCT / CRI** (color), then confirm wattage is low.

| Old incandescent (approx.) | Target LED lumens | Typical LED wattage range |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 40W | ~450 lm | ~5–7W |
| 60W | ~800 lm | ~5–12W (ultra-efficient packs can land near ~5W) |
| 75W | ~1100 lm | ~11–15W |
| 100W | ~1600 lm | ~15–20W |

Always read the package: a “60W replacement” should land near **800 lumens**, not merely say “60W” in big type while delivering a dim 450 lm lamp.

### Color temperature quick picks

- **2700K–3000K:** Warm, residential living rooms / bedrooms.
- **3000K–3500K:** Neutral warm for kitchens and baths.
- **4000K+:** Cooler task / garage / utility — fine if you like it; jarring if you don’t.

Prefer **CRI 80+** (90+ nicer for kitchens and makeup lighting). Skip mystery no-name bulbs that omit lumens and CRI.

## Room-by-room: what to swap first (and what can wait)

### Prioritize (hours every day)

1. **Kitchen ceilings and under-cabinet lights** — often the longest daily runtime in the house.
2. **Living room lamps and overheads** — long evening hours; match warm CCT so you don’t hate the room and turn everything brighter.
3. **Home office / schoolwork spaces** — daytime hours add up; task lamps beat blasting every can.
4. **Porch and entry lights** left on for safety — confirm damp/wet ratings; indoor A19s die early outdoors.
5. **Garage shop / basement work lights** — long DIY sessions; daylight 5000K multipacks belong *here*, not in bedrooms.

### Deprioritize (honest do-not-rush list)

- Guest-room closets opened twice a year
- Decorative fixtures you almost never switch on
- Sockets already filled with decent LEDs that match color and don’t flicker
- Specialty bases you haven’t measured (pin bases, low-voltage transformers) until you confirm voltage and form factor

Swap the deprioritized sockets eventually for consistency — don’t pretend they rewrite the bill.

## Buyer decision tree (pick the right pack)

Use this before you cart a bulk box:

1. **Most sockets are standard A19, no dimmer, soft-white preferred?** → Everyday ENERGY STAR soft-white multipack (~800 lm).
2. **Fixtures on wall dimmers?** → Explicitly **dimmable** A19 (warm-glow styles often behave better). Budget for an LED-rated dimmer if a whole room flickers.
3. **Recessed cans / porch floods?** → Correct **BR30** (or BR40) form factor; outdoor/enclosed rating when the fixture needs it — not an indoor A19 jammed in a can.
4. **Garage, laundry, unfinished basement only?** → Bulk **daylight** multipack can be fine; keep it out of living rooms unless you truly want 5000K.
5. **Tempted by smart bulbs for “savings”?** → Buy dumb LEDs first for bill math. Smart bulbs are for schedules, scenes, and remote control. A schedule can help if humans leave lights on — it does not beat a 5–9W dumb LED already in the socket. If the problem is standby AV gear, that’s [smart plugs](/blog/best-smart-plugs-vampire-power/), not Hue.

## Fixture and dimmer gotchas

> **Safety notes:** Kill power at the switch or breaker before opening fixtures. Don’t exceed fixture wattage **and** follow LED-specific enclosure ratings (IC-rated cans, enclosed globes). Use damp/wet-rated lamps outdoors. For GU10/MR16 and pin bases, confirm voltage (line vs. low-voltage transformer). Mis-matched dimmers cause flicker, buzz, or early failure — use LED-compatible dimmers and check the bulb’s dimmable label. Recessed can retrofits must maintain fire/IC ratings; when unsure, use listed retrofit kits or hire an electrician.

Other practical gotchas:

- **Enclosed fixtures:** Only use bulbs rated for enclosed use or they overheat and die early.
- **3-way lamps:** Need 3-way LED designs, not standard dimmable A19s.
- **Photocells / motion sensors:** Cheap LEDs sometimes ghost-glow; buy compatible models.
- **Candelabra / globe aesthetics:** Match shape if the fixture shows the bulb — don’t force an A19 into a chandelier arm.
- **Heat and summer bills:** Incandescent heat is waste indoors in cooling season; LEDs cut that waste. In heating season, that heat was never an efficient furnace substitute — fix setpoints with a sane [thermostat strategy](/blog/best-smart-thermostats-2026/) instead of keeping hot lamps on purpose.

## Pair LEDs with behavior (still free)

- Turn off empty rooms — LEDs are efficient, not free.
- Use task lighting instead of blasting every can at night.
- On [time-of-use rates](/blog/lower-electric-bill-priority-checklist/), decorative lighting in peak windows is still avoidable waste.
- Hunt remaining halogen torchieres; they’re quiet bill villains.
- After sockets are done, cut standby clusters with [smart plugs](/blog/best-smart-plugs-vampire-power/) — a warm AV rack can dwarf a nightlight.
