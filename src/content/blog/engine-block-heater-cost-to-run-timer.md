If you live where winter mornings start below zero, the block heater is one of the most useful things on your car. It keeps the coolant warm so the engine turns over easily, the oil flows right away and the defroster blows warm air before you reach the end of the street. It's also an easy way to waste electricity, because most people plug in when they get home and unplug when they leave, and the heater draws full power the whole time.

A block heater only needs a couple of hours to do its job. Here's what it costs to run per morning and per winter, how much a timer saves, and the timers and cords that hold up in hard cold.

## The short version

- Car block heaters are usually 400 to 600 watts. V8 trucks often use 600 to 1,000 watts, and diesel pickups can be 1,000 to 1,500 watts.
- Two hours of plugging in is enough on most cold mornings. Overnight is five to six times the electricity for almost the same result.
- A 1,000-watt heater plugged in overnight for 100 winter nights costs about $204 at $0.17/kWh. On a two-hour timer it's about $34.
- A $15 to $25 outdoor timer usually pays for itself in the first few weeks of real cold.
- Use an outdoor-rated timer and a cold-weather extension cord, and plug into a GFCI-protected outlet.

Find your real electric rate by dividing your bill's total by the kWh you used. We use $0.17/kWh as a rough US average. Most block heaters have no thermostat of their own, so they draw their full rating for as long as they're plugged in. The wattage is usually stamped on the heater or printed in the owner's manual.

Utilities have been making this point for years. Wisconsin Public Service and We Energies both tell farm customers that a block heater takes only one to two hours to warm a tractor engine, and that a simple timer usually pays for itself in one to three months.

## Why two hours is enough

A block heater warms the coolant, and the coolant warms the block and the oil. In the first hour or two, the engine absorbs most of that heat and its temperature climbs. After that, the engine is losing heat to the cold air about as fast as the heater adds it, so the temperature levels off. Every hour after that point is mostly paying to replace heat that drifts away into the night.

Natural Resources Canada puts it plainly: get an automatic timer that switches the heater on two hours before you drive instead of leaving it plugged in all night, because that's usually all the time needed to warm the coolant and the engine. Woods, which makes one of the common block heater timers, tells buyers to set theirs for two to three hours before departure.

The one time to give it longer is in serious cold. At -20°F and below, or with a big diesel, three or four hours is reasonable. That's still a third of what overnight costs.

## Is it worth plugging in at all?

For most cold-climate drivers, yes. Natural Resources Canada says that at -20°C (-4°F) a block heater can improve overall fuel economy by as much as 10 percent, and that for a single short trip at -25°C (-13°F) the savings can be around 25 percent. Cold starts are also hard on an engine, because thick oil takes longer to reach the parts that need it.

There's a clean-air case too. Anchorage, which deals with winter carbon monoxide build-up, runs a campaign called Plug at 20 that asks drivers to plug in when it's 20°F or colder. The city cites local testing showing that preheating an engine for two to three hours before starting cuts carbon monoxide from the cold start by nearly 60 percent.

Compare it with idling. The U.S. Department of Energy says idling uses about a quarter to a half gallon of fuel an hour, depending on the engine. Fifteen minutes of idling in the driveway costs roughly 20¢ to 40¢ at $3.10 a gallon, and the engine still isn't fully warm. A 400-watt heater on a two-hour timer costs about 14¢ at $0.17/kWh.

## What to do first

1. Find out whether your vehicle already has a block heater. Look for a plug tucked behind the grille or bumper, or a cold-weather package on the window sticker. Many trucks and cars sold in cold states have one from the factory.
2. Find the wattage. It's often on the heater, in the owner's manual, or on the parts listing for your engine.
3. Check the outlet. Outdoor outlets should be GFCI-protected and have an in-use cover that closes over a plugged-in cord. If you don't have an outlet near where you park, have one added rather than running a cord out a window.
4. Get an outdoor timer rated for at least 15 amps and set it to come on two hours before you usually leave, or three to four hours on the coldest days.
5. Use a cold-weather extension cord sized for the heater, and keep the connection off the ground.
6. Test the setup before the first deep freeze. Plug a lamp into the timer to check it switches, and make sure the heater actually warms the engine.

## What not to do

- Don't leave the heater plugged in from evening to morning by default. That's where almost all the wasted money goes.
- Don't use an indoor timer or a light-duty indoor extension cord outdoors.
- Don't use a photocell timer. It turns on at dusk, which is the opposite of what you want.
- Don't load one timer past its rating. Most outdoor plug-in timers are rated 15 amps, or 1,875 watts. Two 1,000-watt heaters on one timer is too much.
- Don't drive off with the cord plugged in. Hang the plug end on the mirror or door handle as a reminder.
- Don't tape over a cracked cord or a melted plug. Replace it.
- Don't cut into coolant hoses or pull a frost plug yourself unless you know what you're doing. Installed block heaters are a mechanic job on most engines.

## Which one should you buy?

If your vehicle already has a block heater and you have an outdoor outlet, buy a timer. Nothing else on this page saves as much.

For one car with the same morning schedule every day, a basic outdoor mechanical timer or the Woods digital block heater timer is all you need. If your schedule changes or you'd like to start the heater from bed when it turns out colder than forecast, the Kasa outdoor smart plug does that from your phone, as long as your Wi-Fi reaches the driveway and it doesn't routinely get colder than its -4°F rating where you live. In colder places than that, stick with a mechanical timer.

If you're using a short, light extension cord now, replace it with a 12-gauge cold-weather cord. It stays flexible well below zero and has headroom for a big truck heater.

If your car has no block heater, the first thing to price is a heater made for your engine, installed by a mechanic. Battery warmers and oil pan heaters are cheap add-ons that help, especially on diesels and older batteries.

And if your car mostly sits, a battery maintainer is the better buy. A weak battery is behind a lot of cold no-starts.

## Setting it up so it works

- Mount the timer above snow level. Anchorage suggests about 3 feet up on a post or the garage wall.
- Set the clock to the real time and check it now and then. Power blips and deep cold can make timer clocks drift.
- Test the timer indoors with a lamp before you rely on it.
- Plug into a GFCI-protected outlet with an in-use cover that closes over the plug.
- Keep the cord connection off the ground, out of puddles and snowbanks, and away from the snowplow's path.
- Route the cord where it won't get pinched in a door or run over.
- Look at the heater cord at the grille every fall. Mice, road salt and slammed hoods damage them.
- Put battery and oil pan heaters on the same timer as the block heater, as long as the total stays under the timer's rating.

## Troubleshooting

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Engine still cold after two hours | Timer clock wrong, heater not getting power, or a failed element | Check the timer with a lamp, then test the heater with a plug-in meter |
| GFCI trips when you plug in | Moisture in the plug or connection, or a failing heater element | Dry and cover the connection; if it keeps tripping, have the heater checked |
| Timer turned on at the wrong time | Clock drifted after a power outage | Reset the clock, and check it after every outage |
| Smart plug offline on cold mornings | Weak Wi-Fi outside, or below its temperature rating | Move the router or add an extender, or switch to a mechanical timer |
| Cord is stiff and cracking | Vinyl cord in hard cold | Replace it with an SJEOW or SJOOW cold-weather cord |
| Electric bill jumped in winter | Heater left plugged in overnight every night | Put it on a timer for two to three hours |
| Breaker trips with two vehicles | Two big heaters on one circuit with other loads | Split them across circuits, or stagger the timer start times |

## Is it worth the money?

If your vehicle has a block heater and you've been plugging in overnight, a timer is one of the best $20 purchases you can make for your electric bill. For a 400-watt car heater it saves about $68 over a 100-night winter. For a big truck heater it can save $150 or more.

If you only plug in a handful of times a year, the savings are smaller, but the timer still means you don't have to go out in the cold at 5 a.m. to plug in.

## Bottom line

Plug in when it's cold enough to matter, put the heater on an outdoor timer set for two to three hours before you leave, and use a cord that stays flexible below zero.
